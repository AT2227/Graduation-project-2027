Subcarrier and Bandwidth Limitations

Every CSI extraction tool imposes a hard ceiling on how much spatial detail you can capture, and this ceiling varies significantly by hardware. If we choose ESP32-based receivers, we are constrained by both the chip's Wi-Fi capability and its serial output bandwidth: testing has shown that 256 subcarriers cause immediate packet loss on ESP32 due to UART throughput bottlenecks, meaning that in practice we are limited to roughly 128 usable subcarriers even though the hardware technically supports more. If we choose Raspberry Pi with Nexmon, we get access to up to 242 subcarriers at 80 MHz bandwidth in theory, but real-world literature reports that only around 114 subcarriers are actually useful for activity/presence recognition, with the rest being unreliable. Older tools like Intel 5300 cap out at just 30 subcarrier groups, which may be too coarse for the localization granularity our project now requires. This means our choice of hardware directly limits the resolution of data available to our ML models, and we need to settle on an option early since switching later would require re-collecting all training data.


Hardware Availability and Discontinuation Risk

Some of the most well-documented and widely-cited CSI tools in the research literature are built on hardware that is no longer manufactured. The Intel 5300 NIC, used in the majority of foundational CSI sensing papers (including the Tokunaga et al. paper we reviewed), was released in 2008 and is effectively discontinued, meaning we would need to source used or old-stock units, and pair them with equally old laptop hardware capable of accepting that NIC, which is a real deployment risk for a project with fixed academic deadlines. This creates a tension: the hardware with the deepest body of supporting research is the hardest to actually acquire and integrate into a modern system, while newer, easier-to-source hardware like ESP32 or PicoScenes-compatible NICs has comparatively less established literature to lean on for troubleshooting or benchmarking against.


Data Quality and Noise Concerns

Different CSI tools are not equally clean in the CSI they produce. Prior research has explicitly noted that Nexmon-based CSI data on Raspberry Pi tends to be noisier than data collected via Intel 5300 or Atheros tools, which is a meaningful concern if we choose the Pi as a primary or sole receiver, since noisy CSI directly degrades classification and localization accuracy and may require more aggressive preprocessing (filtering, DC removal, etc.) to compensate. More generally, all CSI hardware is sensitive to environmental interference — other Wi-Fi networks operating on overlapping channels, Bluetooth devices, microwaves, and even electronic equipment in the room can inject noise into the readings, and since our system depends on detecting subtle amplitude and phase changes caused by a human body, distinguishing real motion-induced signal changes from environmental RF noise will be an ongoing challenge throughout data collection and model training.


Multi-Node Synchronization Difficulties

Because localization requires comparing data from multiple receivers simultaneously, any timing misalignment between nodes directly corrupts our data. Each receiver node has its own internal clock, and without careful synchronization (e.g., NTP), timestamps across nodes can drift by tens of milliseconds, which is enough to make simultaneous readings represent different physical moments — for instance, comparing one node's snapshot of a person mid-step to another node's snapshot of the person already having landed. This problem is compounded if using a mix of hardware types (e.g., ESP32 nodes and a Raspberry Pi together), since different devices may have different native clock stability, different boot-up timing behavior, and different tools for setting and maintaining synchronized time. Additionally, transmission delay between when a CSI frame is captured at the receiver and when it actually reaches our central laptop (over serial or Wi-Fi) can reintroduce timing errors if we timestamp at arrival rather than at capture, so our pipeline needs to be carefully designed to timestamp as close to the physical measurement as possible.


Physical Placement and Room Geometry Constraints

Multipath propagation — the way Wi-Fi signals bounce off walls, furniture, and other objects before reaching the receiver — means that receiver and transmitter placement significantly affects data quality, and poor placement can create blind spots or regions of the room where signal changes are too subtle to detect reliably. Because we are now attempting localization rather than simple presence detection, receiver placement matters even more: nodes need enough spatial separation and non-collinear positioning to allow triangulation or fingerprinting to work, but our fixed testing room may have physical constraints (furniture, doorways, limited wall space, power outlet locations) that restrict where we can actually place hardware. There is also a phenomenon known as the Fresnel zone that governs how sensitive a given TX-RX link is to a person's exact position relative to that link, meaning some positions in the room may be much easier to detect than others purely due to geometry, regardless of how good our hardware or model is.


Cost and Scalability Tradeoffs

Hardware choice creates a direct tradeoff between cost, resolution, and ease of scaling to multiple nodes. ESP32-based nodes are inexpensive (roughly $5-15 per board) and easy to deploy in multiples, making them attractive for the 3+ receiver setup that localization requires, but each additional Atheros or Intel-based node requires an entire additional laptop or PC (since these are PC-hosted NICs, not standalone microcontrollers), which becomes expensive and physically cumbersome if we want several of them spread around the room. This means that if we want the higher per-node CSI resolution offered by Atheros or Intel-class hardware, we may be forced to compromise on the number of nodes we can practically afford and deploy, which in turn limits localization accuracy — creating a genuine three-way tension between per-node data quality, spatial coverage, and budget/logistics.


Driver, Firmware, and Software Compatibility Issues

Several of these CSI tools require modified firmware or kernel-level driver patches rather than working out of the box, which introduces real technical risk for a student team on a tight timeline. Nexmon CSI historically required specific, older Linux kernel versions (up to only 5.4 for a long period), meaning we cannot simply use the latest Raspberry Pi OS without careful version management, and any accidental system update could break our CSI extraction capability entirely. Similarly, Intel 5300 and Atheros tools rely on custom firmware images and patched wireless drivers that were built for specific, older operating system versions, so getting these tools running on modern laptops may involve significant compatibility troubleshooting, virtual machines, or dedicated legacy hardware set aside solely for data collection. This kind of low-level systems debugging is a real time sink that is easy to underestimate when planning the semester schedule.


Real-Time Performance and Latency Constraints

If the live demo requires near real-time presence/localization feedback, we need to consider whether our chosen hardware can sustain the packet rate and data throughput required without dropping frames, especially once we scale to multiple simultaneous receiver streams being fused together. High subcarrier counts and high sampling rates increase the risk of packet loss (as already observed with ESP32 at high subcarrier settings), and any dropped or delayed packets during live operation would degrade the smoothness and reliability of our detection system in front of the demo audience. Balancing subcarrier resolution, sampling rate, and number of simultaneous nodes against what our laptop can process and display in real time will require empirical testing once hardware is chosen, rather than something we can fully predict on paper.
